# Compliance Basics — EIN, Licenses, Permits, Registered Agent, Filings

Educational reference for FounderLex. Ground compliance explanations in this material; requirements vary significantly by state, city, and industry — don't invent specific fees or deadlines for a jurisdiction not covered here.

## EIN (Employer Identification Number)

An EIN is essentially a Social Security number for a business — issued by the IRS, used to open a business bank account, hire employees, and file business taxes. It's **free** and takes about 10 minutes to get directly at IRS.gov. Never pay a third-party service to obtain one; some services charge a fee for something the IRS provides free.

## Registered Agent

Most states require an LLC or corporation to designate a **registered agent** — a person or company with a physical address in the state of formation, responsible for receiving official legal and government correspondence (like being served in a lawsuit) on the business's behalf. Founders can often serve as their own registered agent if they have a physical address in the state, or use a commercial registered-agent service (commonly $50–$300/year) — especially useful if the founders don't live in the state of formation (e.g., forming a Delaware C-Corp while living elsewhere).

## Business Licenses and Permits

Separate from forming an LLC/corporation, many businesses need specific licenses or permits to legally operate — these vary enormously by industry, city, and state. Common examples: a general business license from the city/county, a seller's permit for collecting sales tax, health permits for food-related businesses, or professional licenses for regulated services. There's no single national answer here — founders should check their specific city/county and state requirements for their specific business activity, or consult a local attorney/accountant if unsure.

## State vs. Federal Filings

- **State-level:** forming the LLC/corporation itself (filed with the Secretary of State), most business licenses, sales tax registration.
- **Federal-level:** EIN (IRS), federal tax filings, and — for nonprofits — the 501(c)(3) exemption application (see nonprofit basics).

## Ongoing Compliance Once Formed

Forming a business isn't a one-time event — most states require some form of ongoing filing to keep the entity in good standing, such as an annual report or franchise tax filing (varies by state and entity type). Missing these can result in penalties or even administrative dissolution of the business. Founders should check their specific state's requirements after formation, not just at the time of filing.

## When to Get a Lawyer or Accountant

Exactly which licenses, permits, and ongoing filings apply is highly specific to industry, location, and business activity. General compliance basics explain the categories to be aware of, but a founder should confirm specific requirements with their state/city government resources, or a local attorney/accountant, rather than assume the general categories above are a complete list for their situation.
