# Consulting Basics — NDAs, SOW vs. PO, IP Split, Worker Classification

Educational reference for FounderLex. Ground consulting-specific explanations in this material; don't invent fees, rates, or contract specifics beyond what's written here.

## Mutual NDA vs. One-Way NDA

In consulting, both sides often exchange sensitive information before any deal is signed — the client shares business details to get a proposal, the consultant shares methodology to explain their approach. A **mutual NDA** protects both parties as both a disclosing and receiving party, which is the standard choice for consulting relationships. A **one-way (unilateral) NDA** only protects whichever party is disclosing information — appropriate when only one side is actually sharing anything sensitive.

## Statement of Work (SOW) — the Most Important Consulting Document

An SOW is the document that actually locks in a client engagement. It should specify:

- **Scope** — exactly what work is being done (and, just as important, what's explicitly out of scope, to prevent scope creep — see below)
- **Deliverables** — the specific things being handed over (a report, a piece of software, a strategy document)
- **Timeline** — start date, milestones, and end date
- **Resources** — who on the consulting side is doing the work
- **Rates and payment terms** — how much, how billed, when due
- **IP ownership** — who owns the deliverable vs. the underlying methods used to create it (see the IP split explanation below)

## Purchase Order (PO)

A PO is a simpler document used when a consulting firm is buying something from a vendor or supplier rather than delivering services to a client — it locks in what's being purchased, at what price, and by when.

## Independent Contractor Agreement

Used when a consulting firm hires someone (a subcontractor, freelancer) to help deliver client work. The IP-assignment clause is essential here too — without it, the person hired may retain ownership of the work they created, which is a problem if that work is what the firm is delivering to its own client.

## The Consulting IP Split (Important Distinction)

In a standard contractor relationship, everything the contractor creates typically goes to the client. Consulting works differently, and this distinction should be explained clearly and spelled out in every consulting contract:

- **The consultant keeps their pre-existing tools, frameworks, and methodologies** — the general expertise and approach they bring to every client, developed over time and reused across engagements.
- **The client owns the specific work product** created for them under this engagement — the particular report, strategy, codebase, or analysis they paid for.

Without this split written into the contract explicitly, disputes arise over whether a consultant can reuse a framework with their next client, or whether a client can claim ownership of a consultant's general methodology.

## Scope Creep

Scope creep is when a client's requests gradually expand beyond what was originally agreed in the SOW, without a corresponding adjustment to timeline or payment. The main defense is a well-specified SOW (see above) plus a **change order process** — a lightweight way to formally add scope with an agreed rate/timeline adjustment, rather than absorbing extra work for free.

## Worker Classification (Contractor vs. Employee)

Consulting firms hiring subcontractors need to be careful about whether that person is legally an independent contractor or should actually be classified as an employee. Misclassification carries real legal and tax risk (back taxes, penalties). Factors regulators generally look at include how much control the hiring firm has over how/when the work is done, whether the person works for other clients too, and whether they use their own equipment/methods. This is a fact-specific determination — when there's genuine ambiguity, a consulting firm should talk to an employment attorney or accountant rather than guess.

## Business Structure for Consulting Firms

Most consulting firms start as **LLCs** — simpler paperwork, pass-through taxation, and personal liability protection. A **C-Corp** typically only makes sense for a consulting firm that plans to raise outside investment, which is uncommon for most consulting/services businesses. Registration happens through the state's Secretary of State office.
