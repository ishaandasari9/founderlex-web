# Contracts Basics — What Makes a Contract, Key Clauses, Common Types

Educational reference for FounderLex. Ground contract explanations in this material; don't invent enforceability outcomes for a specific real contract — that always requires an attorney reviewing the actual document and facts.

## What Makes a Contract Legally Binding

A contract generally needs three things to be enforceable:

- **Offer** — one party proposes specific terms.
- **Acceptance** — the other party agrees to those terms.
- **Consideration** — something of value is exchanged by both sides (money, services, a promise to do or not do something). A one-sided promise with nothing given in return generally isn't an enforceable contract.

Contracts don't have to be long or formal to be binding — a short signed agreement, or even certain email exchanges confirming terms, can be enforceable. That said, for anything with real stakes (equity, IP, significant money), a clear written document is strongly preferred over an informal or verbal agreement, because proving what was actually agreed to becomes very difficult without one.

## Verbal Agreements

Verbal agreements can be legally binding in many situations, but they are extremely difficult to enforce because there's no reliable record of the actual terms. This is exactly the trap that catches founders in disputes like "we agreed 33% each" with nothing signed — without a written agreement, what was actually promised, and under what conditions, becomes a matter of dueling memories rather than a document a court can just read.

## Key Clauses to Look For (or Include)

- **Scope/deliverables** — precisely what each side is providing.
- **Payment terms** — amount, schedule, and what triggers payment.
- **Term and termination** — how long the agreement lasts, and how either side can end it early.
- **IP ownership** — who owns work product created under the contract (see IP basics for the general default rules and the consulting-specific split).
- **Confidentiality** — whether either side must keep information private (sometimes a standalone NDA instead).
- **Governing law** — which state's law applies if there's a dispute.
- **Dispute resolution** — how disagreements get resolved (informal discussion, mediation, binding arbitration, or litigation as a last resort) — see the Founders' Agreement template's dispute-resolution section for the typical progression used in startup context.
- **Limitation of liability** — caps on how much one party can be sued for under the contract.

## Breach of Contract

A breach occurs when one party fails to do what the contract required — failing to pay, failing to deliver, or violating a specific term like confidentiality. Whether a specific breach entitles the other side to damages, termination, or another remedy depends on the contract's exact language and the applicable state law — this is fact-specific and not something to resolve by general guidance; an attorney should evaluate an actual dispute.

## Common Contract Types a Founder Encounters

- **NDA** — protects confidential information shared between parties (see IP basics).
- **Founders' Agreement** — governs equity, roles, vesting, and what happens if a founder leaves.
- **Independent Contractor Agreement** — governs a hired contractor's work and, critically, assigns IP in that work to the hiring company.
- **Statement of Work (SOW)** — locks in scope, deliverables, timeline, and rates for a consulting engagement.
- **Terms of Service** — the contract between a company and its users governing use of a product/service.
- **Purchase Order (PO)** — locks in a specific purchase from a vendor.

## When to Get a Lawyer

General contract fundamentals are useful for understanding what a document is trying to do, but assessing whether a *specific* real contract is enforceable, who's likely to win a dispute, or how to draft precise legal language for a high-stakes agreement requires a licensed attorney reviewing the actual facts and document — not a general framework.
