# Nonprofit Basics — 501(c)(3) Path, Bylaws, Compliance

Educational reference for FounderLex. Ground nonprofit explanations in this material; don't invent fees, deadlines, or IRS specifics beyond what's written here.

## Section 1 — What "Nonprofit" and 501(c)(3) Actually Mean

A nonprofit is a corporation organized for public benefit rather than to enrich its founders or owners. "Nonprofit" doesn't mean the organization can't make money, pay staff, or have revenue — it means the organization cannot *distribute profits to its founders, officers, or directors* the way a for-profit company distributes profits to shareholders. Any surplus goes back into the mission.

501(c)(3) is a specific **federal tax exemption** granted by the IRS, layered on top of a state-level nonprofit corporation. Forming a nonprofit corporation with your state and getting 501(c)(3) status from the IRS are two separate steps, done in that order.

## Section 2 — Required IRS Clauses (Verbatim Language)

The IRS requires two specific clauses to appear in a 501(c)(3) organization's Articles of Incorporation, and the language must be precise:

- **Organizational purpose clause** — limits the organization's purposes to those described in Internal Revenue Code Section 501(c)(3) (charitable, religious, educational, scientific, literary, etc.) and states that no substantial part of its activities will involve lobbying or political campaign intervention.
- **Dissolution clause** — states that if the organization ever dissolves, its remaining assets will be distributed to another 501(c)(3) organization or for another exempt purpose — never to the founders, directors, or members personally.

Getting this language wrong or omitting it is one of the most common reasons a 501(c)(3) application gets delayed or rejected. The exact approved wording should come from the IRS's own guidance or a nonprofit attorney/CPA — FounderLex can draft a starting-point Articles document, but the final language should be reviewed by a professional before filing.

## Section 3 — What Bylaws Must Cover

Bylaws are the internal operating rules for the organization — separate from the publicly-filed Articles of Incorporation. At minimum, Bylaws typically cover:

- Number of board members (minimum and maximum) and how they're elected or appointed
- Officer roles (e.g., Chair, Secretary, Treasurer) and their responsibilities
- How and when board meetings happen (regular meeting frequency, notice required, quorum)
- How decisions are made (voting thresholds for ordinary vs. major decisions)
- How board members can be removed and how vacancies are filled
- Fiscal year start/end

## Section 4 — Conflict of Interest Policy

The IRS specifically asks whether an organization has adopted a Conflict of Interest Policy on Form 1023 (the federal tax-exemption application) — while not always a strict legal requirement, its absence is a red flag to reviewers and to funders.

A Conflict of Interest Policy governs what happens when a board member (or officer) has a personal financial interest in a decision the board is making — for example, the board considering a contract with a company the board member owns. The policy should require:

- Disclosure of the conflict before the relevant discussion or vote
- The conflicted board member recusing themselves from discussion and voting on that specific matter
- Documentation of the disclosure and recusal in meeting minutes

Most 501(c)(3) organizations also have board members complete an **annual conflict-of-interest questionnaire** confirming they've disclosed any financial interests, board memberships elsewhere, or family relationships that could create a conflict.

## Section 5 — EIN and Form 1023 Overview

- **EIN (Employer Identification Number):** Get this free directly from IRS.gov — it takes about 10 minutes online. Never pay a third-party service for this; it's always free from the IRS. *(Process details can change — confirm the current application steps at irs.gov before starting.)*
- **Form 1023 / 1023-EZ:** This is the federal application for 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status, filed after the nonprofit corporation is formed at the state level and has its EIN. See Section 6 for which version applies.

## Section 6 — Form 1023 vs. Form 1023-EZ Eligibility

*Source: IRS Publication 557 ("Tax-Exempt Status for Your Organization") and the Form 1023-EZ Eligibility Worksheet in the Form 1023-EZ Instructions, both at irs.gov.*

- **Form 1023-EZ** — the simplified, shorter application. Eligible only for smaller organizations: generally those projecting **$50,000 or less** in annual gross receipts for each of the next 3 years, and with total assets of **$250,000 or less**. Filing fee is $275. Filed online through Pay.gov.
- **Form 1023 (full form)** — required for organizations that don't meet the 1023-EZ thresholds, or that have more complex structures. Filing fee is $600. Requires substantially more detail: full narrative of activities, financial projections, governance documents.
- **Typical processing time:** IRS processing times fluctuate significantly with staffing and backlog — Form 1023-EZ has historically been much faster (often weeks) than the full Form 1023 (often several months to longer). Treat any specific timeframe as a rough planning estimate, not a guarantee.

> ⚠️ **These dollar thresholds, fees, and processing times are exactly the kind of detail the IRS updates over time. Verify the current amounts and eligibility worksheet directly at irs.gov before relying on them or filing anything.** This applies to every dollar figure and threshold in this section.

## Section 7 — Board Composition and Fiduciary Duties

Nonprofits are legally required to have a board of directors — even a small nonprofit needs at least a few people (commonly a minimum of 3), and board members don't have to be paid.

Board members owe the organization three fiduciary duties:

- **Duty of care** — actively participate in decisions, review materials, act with the diligence a reasonably prudent person would use
- **Duty of loyalty** — act in the organization's best interest, not personal interest (this is what the Conflict of Interest Policy in Section 4 is designed to protect)
- **Duty of obedience** — ensure the organization stays true to its stated mission and complies with applicable laws

Independent directors (board members with no financial stake or employment relationship with the organization) matter to the IRS as evidence the organization is genuinely independent and not effectively controlled by a single insider for personal benefit.

## Section 8 — Ongoing Compliance

501(c)(3) status isn't a one-time filing — it comes with ongoing obligations:

- **Form 990** — an annual information return filed with the IRS. Which version depends on the organization's gross receipts and assets:
  - **Form 990-N** (the "e-Postcard") — for the smallest organizations, those with gross receipts **normally $50,000 or less**. This is the one a small student nonprofit under $50,000 a year files.
  - **Form 990-EZ** — for organizations with gross receipts **under $200,000** and total assets **under $500,000**.
  - **Full Form 990** — for larger organizations, gross receipts **$200,000 or more** or total assets **$500,000 or more**.
  These dollar thresholds can change over time, so confirm the current figures at irs.gov. The deadline is the 15th day of the 5th month after the fiscal year ends (e.g., May 15 for a calendar-year organization).
- **State charitable solicitation registration** — most states require a nonprofit to register **before** soliciting donations from the public in that state — through a website donation button, events, direct mail, or social media fundraising — not just before receiving the money. Requirements and forms vary by state; this needs to be checked state-by-state, especially if fundraising online (which can reach donors in many states at once).
- **Meeting minutes** — boards should keep written minutes of meetings, especially votes, as part of good governance and IRS documentation expectations.

## Section 9 — Common Mistakes

- **The three-year Form 990 auto-revocation rule:** If an organization fails to file its required Form 990 for **three consecutive years**, the IRS automatically revokes its tax-exempt status — no warning letter required beyond standard notices. Reinstatement requires re-applying, which can take months and, in some cases, paying back fees or filing retroactive returns. This is one of the most common and most avoidable ways nonprofits lose their status.
- Soliciting donations publicly before completing state charitable-solicitation registration.
- Treating the state-level nonprofit corporation filing as equivalent to having 501(c)(3) status — they are two separate steps.
- Omitting or mis-wording the required IRS purpose/dissolution clauses in the Articles (see Section 2).

## Section 10 — Donation Acknowledgment Letters (Written Substantiation Requirements)

When a nonprofit receives a donation, the donor's ability to claim a tax deduction depends on the nonprofit giving them the right paperwork — this is a rule about what the *donor* needs, not something the nonprofit files with the IRS itself.

- **The $250 threshold (IRC § 170(f)(8)):** For any single contribution of **$250 or more**, the donor cannot claim a tax deduction unless the nonprofit gives them a **contemporaneous written acknowledgment**. "Contemporaneous" means the donor must have it in hand by the earlier of: the date they file their tax return, or the due date (including extensions) for that return.
- **Required elements of the acknowledgment** (per IRS Publication 1771):
  1. The organization's name.
  2. The amount of a cash contribution, **or** a description (not a dollar value) of a non-cash contribution.
  3. A statement that no goods or services were provided by the organization in return for the contribution — **if that was the case**.
  4. If the organization *did* provide something back (a dinner, a gift, event tickets), a description and good-faith estimate of its value, and a statement that the deduction is limited to the amount given minus that value. This "quid pro quo" disclosure is separately required under IRC § 6115 whenever a single payment over $75 is partly a donation and partly payment for goods or services.
  5. If the only benefit provided was an intangible religious benefit, a statement saying so (no value estimate needed in that case).
- **Best practice vs. legal requirement:** The $250 threshold is the legal trigger, but most nonprofits send an acknowledgment letter for every donation regardless of size — it's good donor relations and avoids tracking which donations cross the line.
- **What NOT to include:** The nonprofit does not need to record or report the donor's Social Security number or other tax ID on the acknowledgment.

## Section 11 — IRS Filing Sequence, Step by Step

This section ties together Sections 1, 2, 5, 6, and 8 into the order a founder actually does them in. It doesn't repeat the numbers already stated in those sections — see the cross-references below for the specifics. This is **procedural filing guidance only**: what the steps are and what order they go in, not tax strategy, and not a substitute for a nonprofit attorney or CPA actually preparing and reviewing the filing.

**(a) Form the nonprofit corporation at the state level.** File Articles of Incorporation with your state's Secretary of State (the same agency that handles LLC and corporation filings, using a nonprofit-specific form). The Articles must include the IRS-required purpose and dissolution clauses verbatim — see Section 2. This step happens *before* anything involving the IRS.

**(b) Get an EIN from the IRS.** Free, directly from irs.gov, roughly 10 minutes online — see Section 5. Do this after the state corporation exists (you'll need basic entity details to apply). Never pay a third-party site for this.

**(c) Decide: Form 1023 or Form 1023-EZ.** This is an eligibility question, not a preference — see Section 6 for the gross-receipts and asset thresholds that determine which form applies. **This is a determination FounderLex can help you understand, but the actual eligibility call and the filing itself should go through a CPA or nonprofit attorney** — getting this wrong (or getting the required language wrong within it) can delay tax-exempt status by months.

**(d) File the chosen form with the IRS.** Both are filed online. Pay the filing fee (Section 6) at the time of filing. Keep a copy of everything submitted — you'll need it for future reference and for state-level charitable registration in some states.

**(e) After approval, ongoing compliance begins.** Tax-exempt status isn't a one-time achievement — it comes with recurring obligations: the annual Form 990 (see Section 8), state charitable solicitation registration before public fundraising (see Section 8), and keeping board meeting minutes. Missing three consecutive years of Form 990 filings triggers automatic revocation (see Section 9).

**Quick checklist (for reference, not a substitute for the steps above):**
- [ ] File state Articles of Incorporation with required IRS purpose/dissolution clauses
- [ ] Get a free EIN at irs.gov
- [ ] Determine 1023 vs. 1023-EZ eligibility (confirm with a CPA/attorney)
- [ ] Adopt Bylaws and a Conflict of Interest Policy (needed for the 1023 application)
- [ ] File the chosen form and pay the fee
- [ ] After approval: track Form 990 deadlines and any state charitable-registration requirements

**What this section is not:** It is not tax strategy — it does not address minimizing taxes, choosing a fiscal year for tax advantage, or any tax election. Questions like that go to a CPA or tax attorney, not this guidance.
