# Nonprofit Basics — 501(c)(3) Path, Bylaws, Compliance

Educational reference for FounderLex. Ground nonprofit explanations in this material; don't invent fees, deadlines, or IRS specifics beyond what's written here.

## Section 1 — What "Nonprofit" and 501(c)(3) Actually Mean

A nonprofit is a corporation organized for public benefit rather than to enrich its founders or owners. "Nonprofit" doesn't mean the organization can't make money, pay staff, or have revenue — it means the organization cannot *distribute profits to its founders, officers, or directors* the way a for-profit company distributes profits to shareholders. Any surplus goes back into the mission.

501(c)(3) is a specific **federal tax exemption** granted by the IRS, layered on top of a state-level nonprofit corporation. Forming a nonprofit corporation with your state and getting 501(c)(3) status from the IRS are two separate steps, done in that order.

## Section 2 — Required IRS Clauses (Verbatim Language)

The IRS requires two specific clauses to appear in a 501(c)(3) organization's Articles of Incorporation, and the language must be precise:

- **Organizational purpose clause** — limits the organization's purposes to those described in Internal Revenue Code Section 501(c)(3) (charitable, religious, educational, scientific, literary, etc.) and states that no substantial part of its activities will involve lobbying or political campaign intervention.
- **Dissolution clause** — states that if the organization ever dissolves, its remaining assets will be distributed to another 501(c)(3) organization or for another exempt purpose — never to the founders, directors, or members personally.

Getting this language wrong or omitting it is one of the most common reasons a 501(c)(3) application gets delayed or rejected. The exact approved wording should come from the IRS's own guidance or a nonprofit attorney/CPA — FounderLex can draft a starting-point Articles document, but the final language should be reviewed by a professional before filing.

## Section 3 — What Bylaws Must Cover

Bylaws are the internal operating rules for the organization — separate from the publicly-filed Articles of Incorporation. At minimum, Bylaws typically cover:

- Number of board members (minimum and maximum) and how they're elected or appointed
- Officer roles (e.g., Chair, Secretary, Treasurer) and their responsibilities
- How and when board meetings happen (regular meeting frequency, notice required, quorum)
- How decisions are made (voting thresholds for ordinary vs. major decisions)
- How board members can be removed and how vacancies are filled
- Fiscal year start/end

## Section 4 — Conflict of Interest Policy

The IRS specifically asks whether an organization has adopted a Conflict of Interest Policy on Form 1023 (the federal tax-exemption application) — while not always a strict legal requirement, its absence is a red flag to reviewers and to funders.

A Conflict of Interest Policy governs what happens when a board member (or officer) has a personal financial interest in a decision the board is making — for example, the board considering a contract with a company the board member owns. The policy should require:

- Disclosure of the conflict before the relevant discussion or vote
- The conflicted board member recusing themselves from discussion and voting on that specific matter
- Documentation of the disclosure and recusal in meeting minutes

Most 501(c)(3) organizations also have board members complete an **annual conflict-of-interest questionnaire** confirming they've disclosed any financial interests, board memberships elsewhere, or family relationships that could create a conflict.

## Section 5 — EIN and Form 1023 Overview

- **EIN (Employer Identification Number):** Get this free directly from IRS.gov — it takes about 10 minutes online. Never pay a third-party service for this; it's always free from the IRS.
- **Form 1023 / 1023-EZ:** This is the federal application for 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status, filed after the nonprofit corporation is formed at the state level and has its EIN. See Section 6 for which version applies.

## Section 6 — Form 1023 vs. Form 1023-EZ Eligibility

- **Form 1023-EZ** — the simplified, shorter application. Eligible only for smaller organizations: generally those projecting **$50,000 or less** in annual gross receipts for each of the next 3 years, and with total assets of **$250,000 or less**. Filing fee is $275.
- **Form 1023 (full form)** — required for organizations that don't meet the 1023-EZ thresholds, or that have more complex structures. Filing fee is $600. Requires substantially more detail: full narrative of activities, financial projections, governance documents.

## Section 7 — Board Composition and Fiduciary Duties

Nonprofits are legally required to have a board of directors — even a small nonprofit needs at least a few people (commonly a minimum of 3), and board members don't have to be paid.

Board members owe the organization three fiduciary duties:

- **Duty of care** — actively participate in decisions, review materials, act with the diligence a reasonably prudent person would use
- **Duty of loyalty** — act in the organization's best interest, not personal interest (this is what the Conflict of Interest Policy in Section 4 is designed to protect)
- **Duty of obedience** — ensure the organization stays true to its stated mission and complies with applicable laws

Independent directors (board members with no financial stake or employment relationship with the organization) matter to the IRS as evidence the organization is genuinely independent and not effectively controlled by a single insider for personal benefit.

## Section 8 — Ongoing Compliance

501(c)(3) status isn't a one-time filing — it comes with ongoing obligations:

- **Form 990** — an annual information return filed with the IRS. Which version (990-N, 990-EZ, or full 990) depends on the organization's gross receipts and assets; smaller organizations file simpler versions. Deadline is the 15th day of the 5th month after the fiscal year ends (e.g., May 15 for a calendar-year organization).
- **State charitable solicitation registration** — most states require a nonprofit to register **before** soliciting donations from the public in that state — through a website donation button, events, direct mail, or social media fundraising — not just before receiving the money. Requirements and forms vary by state; this needs to be checked state-by-state, especially if fundraising online (which can reach donors in many states at once).
- **Meeting minutes** — boards should keep written minutes of meetings, especially votes, as part of good governance and IRS documentation expectations.

## Section 9 — Common Mistakes

- **The three-year Form 990 auto-revocation rule:** If an organization fails to file its required Form 990 for **three consecutive years**, the IRS automatically revokes its tax-exempt status — no warning letter required beyond standard notices. Reinstatement requires re-applying, which can take months and, in some cases, paying back fees or filing retroactive returns. This is one of the most common and most avoidable ways nonprofits lose their status.
- Soliciting donations publicly before completing state charitable-solicitation registration.
- Treating the state-level nonprofit corporation filing as equivalent to having 501(c)(3) status — they are two separate steps.
- Omitting or mis-wording the required IRS purpose/dissolution clauses in the Articles (see Section 2).
