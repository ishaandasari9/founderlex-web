# Business Structures — Sole Prop, Partnership, LLC, S-Corp, C-Corp

Educational reference for FounderLex. Ground business-structure explanations in this material; don't invent state-specific fees or timelines beyond the general ranges given here.

## Sole Proprietorship

No separate legal entity exists — the business *is* the individual. There's no liability protection: if the business is sued or owes money, creditors can reach the owner's personal savings, home, and other assets. It requires no formal registration to start (though local licenses/permits may still be required). Reasonable for zero-risk early experiments, but risky once there are real customers, real revenue, or co-founders involved. The natural next step is forming an LLC.

## General Partnership

Functions like a sole proprietorship but for two or more people. Same personal-liability exposure as a sole proprietorship, plus each partner can be personally on the hook for the *other* partner's business decisions and debts, without having agreed to them individually. Almost always better to form an LLC instead and put a written agreement (a Founders' Agreement, for a product company) in place among the partners.

## LLC (Limited Liability Company)

The default choice for most small startups and consulting firms. An LLC creates a legal separation ("wall") between the business and the owners' personal assets — a properly maintained LLC generally protects personal savings/property from business debts and lawsuits. Taxation is "pass-through" by default: profits flow to the owners' personal tax returns rather than being taxed separately at the company level. Paperwork and ongoing compliance are moderate — lighter than a corporation, heavier than a sole proprietorship. Best fit for: most product startups not planning to raise venture capital, most consulting firms, and most small businesses generally. Formed by filing with the state's Secretary of State office; typical cost is roughly $50–$500 depending on the state, and filing usually takes a few days to a few weeks.

## S-Corporation

Not a separate type of legal entity — it's a **tax election** made on top of an existing LLC or corporation, filed with the IRS. Its main purpose is reducing self-employment tax once the business is consistently profitable, by allowing the owner to split income between salary and distributions. It does not change or add liability protection beyond what the underlying LLC or corporation already provides. Because the tax mechanics and thresholds are specific, this should be discussed with an accountant before electing S-Corp status.

## C-Corporation

A fully separate legal entity, distinct from its owners, capable of issuing stock and stock options to founders, employees, and investors. This is the standard structure expected by venture capital investors — almost always specifically a **Delaware** C-Corp, because Delaware's corporate law is well-established and predictable, and most investors' standard paperwork assumes it. Downsides: the most complex and most expensive structure to form and maintain, and potential "double taxation" (the corporation pays tax on its profits, and shareholders pay tax again on dividends). Setting up a C-Corp correctly — especially the stock structure and any 83(b) election timing — should go through a lawyer rather than being done as a DIY filing. If founder stock is subject to vesting, an 83(b) election (26 U.S.C. § 83(b)) must be filed with the IRS **within 30 days of the stock grant** to lock in today's (usually near-zero) value instead of being taxed at each vesting date — this deadline cannot be extended, so confirm it with a lawyer or accountant immediately after any grant, not after the window closes.

## Choosing and Registering

- If not yet registered and there are real customers, real money, or co-founders involved, forming an LLC is usually the right next step for liability protection.
- If planning to raise venture capital or grant equity broadly to employees, a Delaware C-Corp is what investors will expect — but this is a decision worth making with a lawyer, since converting structures later (e.g., LLC to C-Corp) adds cost and complexity.
- Registration in any case happens through the relevant state's Secretary of State office; cost and processing time vary by state.
