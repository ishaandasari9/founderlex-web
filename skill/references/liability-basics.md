# Liability Basics — Personal vs. Business Liability

Educational reference for FounderLex. Ground liability explanations in this material; don't invent case outcomes or state-specific rules beyond what's written here.

## What "Liability" Means Here

Liability is legal responsibility for a debt, a broken promise (breach of contract), or harm caused to someone else. The core question for a founder is: **if the business is sued or owes money, can the people who lose the lawsuit reach the founders' personal assets** — savings, a car, a house — or only the business's own assets?

## Personal Liability (Sole Proprietorship / General Partnership)

Without a separate legal entity, the founder(s) *are* the business in the eyes of the law. There is no wall between business debts/lawsuits and personal assets. A customer who sues the business, a landlord chasing unpaid rent, or a vendor owed money can all pursue the founder's personal savings and property directly. In a general partnership, this exposure extends further: each partner can be personally liable for the *other* partner's business decisions and debts too.

## Business Liability (LLC / Corporation)

Forming an LLC or corporation creates a legal separation between the business and its owners. Done properly, this generally means creditors and plaintiffs can reach the business's assets but not the owners' personal assets — this is the core reason most founders form an LLC before taking on real customers, money, or risk.

## "Piercing the Corporate Veil" — Why the Wall Isn't Automatic

The liability shield an LLC or corporation provides can be lost if a court finds the owners didn't actually treat the business as a separate entity — for example, mixing personal and business bank accounts, failing to keep basic business records, or using the business as a personal piggy bank. This is sometimes called "piercing the corporate veil." Practical steps to keep the shield intact: keep a separate business bank account, don't pay personal expenses from business funds, keep basic records, and sign contracts in the company's name, not your own.

## Why This Matters for Common Founder Situations

- **Before real customers or revenue:** the risk is often low enough that many founders operate briefly as sole proprietors while validating an idea — but this should be short-lived once there's real activity.
- **Once there are co-founders, paying customers, contractors, or any real money involved:** the personal-liability exposure of operating without an LLC becomes a real risk, not a theoretical one.
- **Signing contracts, hiring, or taking on debt:** always do this in the business's name once an entity exists, not personally — signing personally can undo the liability protection for that specific obligation.

## When to Get a Lawyer

Liability basics explain the general framework, but actual exposure in a specific dispute — whether a contract was properly signed in the company's name, whether the corporate veil could be pierced, how much insurance is appropriate for a given business — depends on facts and state law. A founder facing an actual claim, lawsuit, or significant liability question should talk to a licensed attorney rather than rely on general guidance.
