# DONATION ACKNOWLEDGMENT LETTER

> **What this document does — read this first:** A donor who gives your organization **$250 or more** in a single contribution cannot claim a tax deduction for it unless you give them a **contemporaneous written acknowledgment** — a letter like this one, delivered before they file their tax return. This isn't a form you file with the IRS; it's a receipt you give directly to the donor for their own records. Most nonprofits send this letter for every donation, regardless of size, as good donor relations — but it becomes legally necessary once a single gift reaches $250.
>
> *Grounded in: IRC § 170(f)(8) and IRS Publication 1771. See `references/nonprofit-basics.md` Section 10 for the full rule.*

## 1. Thank the Donor and Record the Contribution

> **What this section does:** States what the organization actually received — this is the core substance the IRS requires. For a cash gift, state the dollar amount. For a non-cash (in-kind) gift, describe what was given, but **do not** assign it a dollar value yourself.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Estimating the value of a non-cash donation (a car, equipment, artwork) on the donor's behalf. The IRS requires the *donor* to determine the value of non-cash contributions (and for larger non-cash gifts, get an independent appraisal) — the organization's job is only to describe what was received, not to put a number on it.
>
> *How to fill this in: For cash, use the exact amount. For non-cash, describe it specifically — "a laptop computer (Dell XPS 15, approximately 2 years old)" rather than just "computer equipment."*

Thank you for your generous contribution to {{organization_name}}. This letter confirms our receipt of:

## 2. The Required Goods-or-Services Statement

> **What this section does:** This is the single most important sentence in the letter for IRS compliance purposes. The IRS requires an explicit statement about whether the donor received anything of value in exchange for the gift — because if they did, only the portion *above* that value is tax-deductible.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Sending a generic thank-you letter with no goods-or-services statement at all when the organization provided a dinner, a gift bag, or event tickets in exchange for the donation (a fundraising gala is the classic example). Omitting this statement — or getting it wrong — can cost the donor their deduction and cause real friction with a donor who later gets audited.
>
> *If the donation was purely a gift with nothing given in return, use Option A. If your organization provided anything in return worth more than a token/insubstantial amount, use Option B — and this triggers the separate "quid pro quo" disclosure rule under IRC § 6115 for any single payment over $75.*
