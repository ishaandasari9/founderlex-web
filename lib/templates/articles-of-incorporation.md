## ARTICLE I — NAME

> **What this section does:** Establishes your organization's legal name — exactly as it will appear in state records, IRS filings, bank accounts, contracts, and all official documents. This must match the name you check for availability with your state before filing.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Not checking name availability before drafting your Articles. Each state's Secretary of State website has a business name search tool. Your organization's name must not already be taken in your state. For a nonprofit, also check whether your proposed name is confusingly similar to an existing nonprofit in your state — even if it's technically "available," a similar name in the same mission space can cause confusion with donors and grantors.
>
> *How to fill this in: Write the full legal name exactly as you want it registered. Nonprofit corporation names typically end in "Inc." or "Incorporated" in most states, but check your specific state's requirements — some states use different required endings for nonprofit corporations. Example: "New Haven Youth Coding Initiative, Inc."*

The name of this corporation is **{{organization_name}}** (the "Corporation").

---

## ARTICLE II — PRINCIPAL OFFICE AND REGISTERED AGENT

> **What this section does:** Identifies where the organization is officially located (principal office) and designates a "registered agent" — the person or service that receives official legal and government mail on behalf of the organization. Every state-registered entity must have a registered agent with a physical address in the state (not a P.O. box in most states).
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Using a founder's home address if they're not comfortable having it in a public filing. Articles of Incorporation are public records. If privacy is a concern, a registered agent service ($50–$150/year) provides a registered address. Law firms that help nonprofits form often serve as the registered agent.
>
> *How to fill this in: The principal office is where you primarily operate — can be an office, a partner organization's address, or a school if you have formal permission. The registered agent must be a person (or registered agent company) physically located in the state of incorporation who is available during business hours.*

The principal office of the Corporation is located at:

**{{principal_office_address}}**, {{state_of_incorporation}}.

The registered agent of the Corporation is **{{registered_agent_name}}**, located at **{{registered_agent_address}}**, {{state_of_incorporation}}.

## ARTICLE III — PURPOSE
### ⚠️ IRS-REQUIRED LANGUAGE — DO NOT ALTER

> **What this section does:** Legally defines the organization's purpose in a way that satisfies the IRS's requirements for 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status. This section contains two components: (1) the IRS-required statutory language — which must appear verbatim — and (2) your organization's specific mission statement, which you write.
>
> **This language is required verbatim for 501(c)(3) eligibility.** The clause below comes directly from IRS Publication 557 ("Tax-Exempt Status for Your Organization"), "Suggested language for corporations." You must not rephrase, summarize, or abbreviate it. The IRS will reject a 501(c)(3) application if the Articles do not contain this language (or its substantial equivalent). Even minor changes can trigger a request for amended Articles — adding months to the process.
>
> *How to fill this in: Keep the required clause exactly as written. Below it, add your organization's specific mission — one or two sentences describing concretely what you do, for whom, and where. The specific mission does not replace the required clause; it supplements it. Example: "More specifically, the Corporation's mission is to provide free coding education and technology access to underserved youth ages 10–18 in the greater New Haven, Connecticut area."*

The Corporation is organized exclusively for charitable, educational, and other purposes as described below.

## ARTICLE IV — NO PRIVATE INUREMENT
### ⚠️ IRS-REQUIRED LANGUAGE — DO NOT ALTER

> **What this section does:** Prohibits the Corporation's earnings from benefiting any individual privately — the founders, board members, officers, or anyone else. This is the legal expression of the core nonprofit principle: no one owns a nonprofit, and no one pockets its earnings. This clause directly enables 501(c)(3) eligibility; its absence will result in rejection of your IRS application.
>
> **What this does NOT prohibit:** Paying reasonable salaries and compensation to employees and staff. A nonprofit can pay its executive director a fair-market salary, reimburse board members for travel expenses, and pay contractors for services. What it prohibits is *distributing profits* — paying out surplus earnings to insiders simply because they founded or lead the organization, the way a for-profit company pays dividends.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Founders who later try to pay themselves above-market compensation or route nonprofit funds to their other businesses. This is called "private inurement" and can result in loss of 501(c)(3) status plus personal "excess benefit transaction" taxes under IRC §4958. The IRS takes this seriously.
>
> **This language is required verbatim. Do not alter it.**

---

**⚠️ REQUIRED IRS LANGUAGE — COPY VERBATIM — DO NOT REPHRASE:**

> "No part of the net earnings of the corporation shall inure to the benefit of, or be distributable to its members, trustees, officers, or other private persons, except that the corporation shall be authorized and empowered to pay reasonable compensation for services rendered and to make payments and distributions in furtherance of the purposes set forth in the purpose clause."

*Source: IRS Publication 557, "Suggested language for corporations." This language must not be altered.*

---

## ARTICLE V — DISSOLUTION
### ⚠️ IRS-REQUIRED LANGUAGE — DO NOT ALTER

> **What this section does:** Guarantees that if the Corporation ever dissolves, its assets will go to another 501(c)(3) organization or a government entity — not to the founders or board members. This clause permanently dedicates the organization's assets to the public benefit and is the third of three IRS-required clauses for 501(c)(3) eligibility.
>
> **Why the IRS requires this:** Without a dissolution clause, founders could dissolve a nonprofit and personally receive the remaining assets — turning tax-deductible donations into personal profit. The dissolution clause closes that loophole. Once this clause is in your Articles and you obtain 501(c)(3) status, the organization's assets are permanently dedicated to public purposes. This is irreversible.
>
> **Important:** Some states have default statutory dissolution rules for nonprofits that satisfy the IRS's requirement automatically. However, including the clause explicitly in your Articles is still recommended — it removes any ambiguity in your 1023 application and provides a clear paper trail.
>
> **This language is required verbatim. Do not alter it.**

---

**⚠️ REQUIRED IRS LANGUAGE — COPY VERBATIM — DO NOT REPHRASE:**

> "Upon the dissolution of this organization, assets shall be distributed for one or more exempt purposes within the meaning of section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, or the corresponding section of any future federal tax code, or shall be distributed to the federal government, or to a state or local government, for a public purpose."

*Source: IRS Publication 557, "Suggested language for corporations." This language must not be altered.*

## ARTICLE VI — BOARD OF DIRECTORS

> **What this section does:** Establishes the governing board — the people legally responsible for the organization. The board holds fiduciary duties (duty of care, loyalty, and obedience), makes major decisions, and is legally accountable for the organization's proper operation. This section sets the basic parameters; your Bylaws govern the details of board composition, terms, meetings, and voting.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Naming a board of all founders and family members. The IRS scrutinizes board independence on Form 1023. A board where the founder controls the majority of seats — especially if the founder is also the paid executive director — raises concerns about private inurement. A majority of independent directors (people with no financial relationship to the organization or to each other) is both best practice and an IRS expectation.
>
> *How to fill this in: Most states require at least 3 directors. Name 3–7 initial directors. Their full legal names and addresses will appear in a public record. After the initial board is named here, your Bylaws govern how future directors are elected.*

The affairs of the Corporation shall be managed by a Board of Directors. The number of directors shall be not fewer than **{{board_min_directors}}** nor more than **{{board_max_directors}}**, as determined by the Board in accordance with the Bylaws.

## ARTICLE VII — LIMITATION OF LIABILITY

> **What this section does:** Limits the personal financial liability of directors for acts taken in good faith on behalf of the organization. This clause is standard in nonprofit Articles and important for attracting qualified board members — people won't serve on a board if they fear personal liability for every organizational decision. Most states permit this limitation by statute; this clause invokes that protection.
>
> *Note: This clause limits monetary liability but does NOT protect directors from liability for intentional misconduct, knowing violations of law, or transactions where the director personally benefited improperly.*

To the fullest extent permitted by the laws of the State of {{state_of_incorporation}}, no director of the Corporation shall be personally liable to the Corporation or its members for monetary damages for any act or omission taken in good faith in the director's capacity as a director, except for: (a) any breach of the director's duty of loyalty to the Corporation; (b) acts or omissions not in good faith or that involve intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law; or (c) any transaction from which the director derived improper personal benefit.
