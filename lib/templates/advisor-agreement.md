## 1. Advisory Services

> **What this section does:** Defines what the Advisor is actually expected to do — and just as importantly, what they're *not* expected to do. Advisors are typically not hands-on: they're not building the product, not doing client work, and not committing fixed hours the way a contractor does. Keeping the scope light and honest is what keeps this relationship classified as an advisory role rather than something closer to employment or contractor work.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Writing a scope that actually describes contractor-level work ("will build the initial product," "will manage the engineering team") and calling it an advisory role to avoid paying a market rate. If the Advisor is doing substantial hands-on work, use an **Independent Contractor Agreement** instead — mischaracterizing real work as "advising" doesn't hold up if a dispute or audit ever looks at what actually happened.
>
> *How to fill this in: Keep it to a short list of light-touch activities. Example: "(1) periodic strategic guidance on product and go-to-market decisions via monthly calls; (2) introductions to potential customers, hires, or investors in the Advisor's network; (3) informal availability for questions between calls." ## 3. Advisor Compensation — Equity Grant

> **What this section does:** States the amount of equity the Advisor will receive in exchange for their advice. This section states the intended grant; it does not, by itself, actually issue the equity — that requires a separate board consent and stock purchase agreement or option grant, prepared with a lawyer, after this Agreement is signed.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Treating this Agreement as if it automatically transfers shares. It doesn't. Equity issuance is a formal corporate action — the board must approve it, paperwork must be filed, and (for a C-corp) the company's 409A valuation and available option pool matter. This Agreement sets the *terms* both sides are agreeing to; the Company's counsel still needs to execute the actual grant correctly.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid — securities law:** Issuing equity to anyone, including an advisor, is a securities transaction and must fit within a securities-law exemption (commonly Rule 701 for compensatory grants, or a state-law exemption). This isn't optional paperwork — get a startup attorney to confirm the grant is structured correctly *before* it's issued, not after.

## 4. Vesting Schedule

> **What this section does:** Sets how the Advisor earns the equity grant over time, rather than owning it all immediately. If the advisory relationship ends early, the Advisor keeps only what has already vested.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Using the same 4-year vesting schedule as a founder or full-time employee. Advisor relationships are lighter-touch and shorter, so advisor vesting is typically shorter too — most commonly vesting monthly over the term of the Agreement, sometimes with a short initial cliff.
>
> **The 83(b) election — do not miss this deadline:** If the Advisor receives restricted stock subject to vesting (rather than an option), the IRS can tax each vesting event as ordinary income based on the shares' value at that time. Filing an **83(b) election within 30 days of the grant** locks in today's value (usually near zero for an early-stage company) instead. **This 30-day window cannot be extended or undone once missed.** The Advisor should talk to a tax advisor about this immediately after the grant, not after the deadline has passed.
>
> *How to decide: A common pattern is monthly vesting over the full advisory term with a 3-month cliff (nothing vests before 3 months; after that, equal monthly installments). Some companies use no cliff at all for advisors, given the lighter commitment.*

## 7. Relationship of the Parties; Termination

> **What this section does:** Clarifies that the Advisor is not an employee — no salary, no benefits, no tax withholding, no right to direct the Advisor's day-to-day activities — and sets out how either side can end the relationship early.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Treating an advisor like a de facto employee — setting fixed hours, requiring exclusivity, or controlling how they do their work. The less control the Company exercises over *how* the Advisor gives advice, the cleaner the non-employee classification. If the Company wants to set fixed hours or direct daily work, that's an employment or contractor relationship, not an advisory one.

The Advisor is an independent advisor, not an employee, partner, or agent of the Company. The Advisor is not entitled to salary, benefits, or tax withholding from the Company, and is responsible for their own taxes on any compensation received.
