# MUTUAL NON-DISCLOSURE AGREEMENT

**This Mutual Non-Disclosure Agreement ("Agreement") is entered into as of {{effective_date}} between:**

- **{{party_1_name}}**, located at {{party_1_address}} ("Party 1"); and

("Mutual" means both sides are protected — each party is both a disclosing party and a receiving party under this Agreement.)

---

## 1. Purpose

> **What this section does:** States exactly *why* the parties are sharing confidential information. A specific purpose clause limits how each side can use what they learn — if you receive confidential information under an NDA for "evaluating a potential partnership," you can't later use that information to compete with the other party. The narrower the purpose, the stronger the protection.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Writing a purpose so broad it covers everything — "general business discussions" or "potential business opportunities." Vague purposes give the receiving party more room to argue that a use was permitted. Be specific: "evaluating a potential acquisition of Party 2," "a potential consulting engagement between the parties," or "determining whether to enter into a co-development agreement for X product."
>
> *How to fill this in: One or two sentences. Name the specific opportunity, transaction, or project the parties are exploring. Examples: "evaluating whether Party 2 will provide market entry consulting services to Party 1" / "exploring a potential co-founder or partnership arrangement" / "Party 1's review of Party 2's technology for a potential licensing deal."*

The parties wish to share information in connection with: {{purpose}} (the "Purpose").

---

## 2. What Counts as Confidential Information

> **What this section does:** Defines the universe of information that is protected under this Agreement. This definition determines what the receiving party must keep secret — so it matters a lot. Too narrow, and important information slips through. Too broad (trying to claim everything is confidential), and courts may not enforce it.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Assuming information is protected only if marked "CONFIDENTIAL." Courts generally enforce a "reasonable person" standard — if a reasonable person would obviously understand that information is sensitive (financial projections, customer lists, unreleased product plans, source code), it's protected even without a label. That said, labeling sensitive documents "Confidential" when you share them is still a good habit — it removes any ambiguity.
>
> *How to fill this in: The definition below is standard. Customize it by adding specific categories relevant to your situation — for example, "including but not limited to [customer data, proprietary algorithms, clinical trial data, acquisition targets]."*

"Confidential Information" means any non-public information disclosed by one party (the "Disclosing Party") to the other (the "Receiving Party"), in any form — written, electronic, oral, or visual — that is:

- **Marked** as confidential, proprietary, or similar at the time of disclosure; **or**
- **Identified** as confidential by the Disclosing Party at or promptly after disclosure; **or**
- **Of a nature** that a reasonable person in the industry would understand to be confidential given the context of the disclosure.

Confidential Information includes, without limitation: business plans, financial projections, revenue figures, customer lists and data, supplier relationships, marketing strategies, technical data, source code, designs, trade secrets, and any information about products, services, or operations not publicly available.

## 3. What Is NOT Confidential

> **What this section does:** Carves out four categories of information that are *not* protected, even if they would otherwise meet the definition in Section 2. These carve-outs are standard in every NDA. They exist because it would be unreasonable — and unenforceable — to require a party to keep secret information they already knew, information that has become public through no fault of theirs, or information they could have independently figured out.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Trying to claim information is confidential after it has already become public. Once information is publicly available through legitimate means (published on your website, disclosed in a press release, available in public records), it can no longer be protected by an NDA. If you accidentally disclose something that was meant to be secret, act immediately — contact the other party and treat it as a breach issue. You cannot retroactively make public information confidential.
>
> *Note: The "independently developed" carve-out (subsection c) means the Receiving Party is protected if they later develop a similar idea on their own, as long as they can prove they didn't use your Confidential Information. Courts look at whether the Receiving Party had established its own independent development before the disclosure.*

Information is not protected under this Agreement if it:

- **(a)** Is or becomes publicly available through no act or omission of the Receiving Party;
- **(b)** Was already known to the Receiving Party before the date of disclosure, as shown by written records predating the disclosure;
- **(c)** Is independently developed by the Receiving Party without use of or reference to the Disclosing Party's Confidential Information; or
- **(d)** Is rightfully received from a third party who had the right to disclose it without restriction.

If the Receiving Party is required by law or court order to disclose Confidential Information, it will (to the extent permitted) promptly notify the Disclosing Party and cooperate to seek a protective order before disclosing.

## 4. Obligations of the Receiving Party

> **What this section does:** States exactly what the Receiving Party must do — and not do — with Confidential Information. These three obligations (use for Purpose only, share only on need-to-know basis, protect with reasonable care) are the core of the NDA. A breach of any one of them entitles the Disclosing Party to legal remedies.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Forwarding confidential information to people who haven't agreed to be bound by confidentiality. "Employees, contractors, and advisors who need to know" means you must ensure those people are under confidentiality obligations themselves — either through their employment agreement, a separate NDA, or another binding obligation. Sending a confidential pitch deck to your roommate "to get their opinion" is a breach of this clause.
>
> *The "reasonable care" standard: This means the same care you use to protect your own confidential information of similar sensitivity — generally, password protection for digital files, limited access, and not leaving materials in unsecured places. It's a practical, not perfect, standard.*

Each party, as Receiving Party, agrees to:

- **(a) Purpose limitation:** Use the Disclosing Party's Confidential Information solely for the Purpose described in Section 1 and for no other purpose.
- **(b) Disclosure restriction:** Not disclose the Confidential Information to any third party without the Disclosing Party's prior written consent, except to the Receiving Party's employees, contractors, and advisors who: (i) have a genuine need to know the information to help evaluate the Purpose, and (ii) are bound by confidentiality obligations at least as protective as this Agreement.
- **(c) Reasonable care:** Protect the Confidential Information using at least the same care the Receiving Party uses to protect its own confidential information of similar sensitivity — and in no event less than reasonable care.

## 5. Term and Duration of Confidentiality Obligations

> **What this section does:** Sets two time limits: how long this Agreement is active, and how long after the Agreement ends the confidentiality obligations continue. These are often different numbers. The Agreement itself might last one year (the evaluation period), but the confidentiality obligations might continue for three years after the last disclosure — because sensitive information shared during that year should stay protected long after the conversation ends.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Using the same number for both the agreement term and the confidentiality obligation. If your NDA says "this Agreement and all obligations expire after 1 year," confidential information shared on day 1 is completely unprotected 12 months later — even if the Receiving Party has done nothing to make it public. The standard practice is a short active term (the evaluation period) plus a longer survival period for the obligations (2–5 years is typical for startup/business context).
>
> **Trade secrets — the special case:** Legally, trade secrets (formulas, source code, customer lists, processes that derive economic value from not being publicly known) can be protected indefinitely — there's no statutory time limit. If the most sensitive thing you're sharing qualifies as a trade secret, consider adding: "Notwithstanding the above, obligations with respect to trade secrets continue for as long as the information remains a trade secret under applicable law." Talk to a lawyer if this applies to your situation.
>
> *How to decide the confidentiality period: 2 years is the minimum most lawyers will accept. 3 years is the most common standard for startup and early-stage business NDAs. 5 years is appropriate for more sensitive technical or commercial information. Longer than 5 years is uncommon for a mutual NDA and may be harder to enforce.*

- **Active term:** This Agreement begins on the effective date and continues for {{nda_term_years}} year(s), unless earlier terminated by written agreement of both parties.
- **Confidentiality obligations survive:** The confidentiality obligations in Sections 2, 3, 4, and 5 survive termination of this Agreement and continue for **{{confidentiality_years}} years** after the date of each disclosure of Confidential Information.

## 6. Return or Destruction

> **What this section does:** Gives the Disclosing Party the right to get its information back — or confirm it's been deleted — when the relationship ends. This is particularly important for physical materials (pitch decks, product samples, proprietary documents) and for digital files that could remain on the Receiving Party's systems indefinitely.
>
> *Practical note: For most startup NDAs, this clause is rarely invoked in practice, but its existence matters. A Receiving Party that knows it may have to return or destroy materials is less likely to keep copies around carelessly. "Certify in writing" is optional language you can add if the information is especially sensitive.*

On written request from the Disclosing Party — or upon expiration or termination of this Agreement — the Receiving Party will promptly return or permanently destroy all Confidential Information in its possession (including copies and notes), and will certify in writing that it has done so if requested.

Notwithstanding the above, the Receiving Party may retain one archival copy solely for legal compliance purposes, subject to the confidentiality obligations in this Agreement.

## 7. No License, No Obligation to Deal

> **What this section does:** Two important protections in one section. (a) Clarifies that sharing information doesn't give the Receiving Party any ownership or usage rights in that information — the Disclosing Party owns it throughout. (b) Makes clear that neither party is committed to any deal just because they signed this NDA. The NDA is for the evaluation stage — signing it doesn't obligate anyone to move forward.
>
> *This section prevents a Receiving Party from arguing: "You showed me how your technology works, so I have an implied license to use it." The answer is: no, you don't — the NDA makes clear the information was shared only for evaluation, not use.*

- **(a) No license:** Nothing in this Agreement grants the Receiving Party any ownership interest in, license to, or other rights in the Disclosing Party's Confidential Information or intellectual property.
- **(b) No commitment:** Neither party is obligated to proceed with any transaction, relationship, or further discussions. Either party may walk away at any time without liability.

## 8. Remedies

> **What this section does:** Establishes that a breach of this NDA can result in an injunction — a court order telling the breaching party to stop. This matters because the standard remedy for breach of contract (money damages) is often inadequate when confidential information has been disclosed — you can't put the information back. Courts regularly grant injunctions for NDA breaches.
>
> *You don't need to add anything here — this language is standard and courts apply it routinely. Its presence tells the Receiving Party you're serious about enforcement.*

Each party acknowledges that a breach of this Agreement may cause irreparable harm for which money damages would be an inadequate remedy, and that the non-breaching party is entitled to seek equitable relief, including injunction and specific performance, in addition to all other remedies available at law or equity.

## 9. Governing Law

> **What this section does:** Picks the state whose law governs any dispute about this Agreement. Each party should use the state where their business is located. When the parties are in different states, this requires a negotiation — the Disclosing Party's state generally gives them a slight advantage if they ever need to enforce the NDA.
>
> *How to fill this in: Use the state where the disclosing party (the one with more to protect) is located. For a mutual NDA where both sides are sharing equally sensitive information, use the state of the party who initiated the conversation or the state where both parties will be working together.*

This Agreement is governed by the laws of the State of {{governing_state}}, without regard to its conflict-of-laws rules.
