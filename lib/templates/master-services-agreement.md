## 1. Purpose and Relationship

> **What this section does:** Explains why this document exists as a separate agreement from a Statement of Work. An MSA sets the *general* terms that apply to every project between these two parties — payment framework, IP ownership rules, confidentiality, liability, termination. A **Statement of Work (SOW)** signed later sets the *project-specific* terms — what work, by when, for how much. You sign the MSA once; you can sign a new short SOW for each new project without re-negotiating the general terms every time.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Signing an MSA and never actually signing a SOW under it. The MSA by itself does not authorize any work or any payment — it's the framework. No work should begin, and no invoice should be sent, until a specific SOW referencing this MSA is signed by both parties.

This MSA establishes the general terms and conditions that will apply to any Statement of Work ("SOW") the parties sign referencing this Agreement. Each SOW will describe the specific scope of services, deliverables, timeline, and fees for that particular engagement.

## 2. How This MSA Works With Statements of Work

> **What this section does:** Sets the rule for what happens if a specific SOW says something different from this MSA — which is common and expected, since project-specific pricing or timelines will vary. The standard approach: the SOW controls project-specific details (scope, price, deliverables, dates); this MSA controls everything else (payment terms, IP, liability, confidentiality) unless the SOW explicitly says otherwise.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Letting a SOW silently override an MSA protection — for example, a Client's SOW template that includes its own IP or liability language that conflicts with this MSA. Read every SOW before signing it, and if a Client's SOW tries to change the deal set here, that needs to be flagged and negotiated explicitly, not accepted by default.

Each SOW signed by both parties and referencing this MSA is incorporated into and governed by this Agreement. If a SOW's terms conflict with this MSA regarding the scope of work, deliverables, timeline, or fees for that specific engagement, the SOW controls. For all other matters — including payment terms, intellectual property, confidentiality, liability, and termination — this MSA controls unless the SOW explicitly and specifically states otherwise.

## 4. Independent Contractor Relationship

> **What this section does:** Confirms Consultant is an independent business, not Client's employee — across every project done under this MSA, not just one. This matters for taxes, benefits, and legal liability, and it's cleaner to state once here than to repeat it in every SOW.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** A Client directing Consultant's day-to-day work as if Consultant were an employee — setting fixed hours, requiring exclusivity, providing equipment, or closely supervising method and manner of work. The more control a Client exercises over *how* the work gets done (versus just *what* gets delivered), the more the relationship starts to look like employment regardless of what this Agreement says on paper.

Consultant is an independent contractor, not an employee, partner, or agent of Client. Consultant is responsible for its own taxes, benefits, insurance, and business expenses. Nothing in this Agreement creates a partnership, joint venture, or employment relationship between the parties.

## 5. Intellectual Property

> **What this section does:** Sets the IP ownership rule that applies to every project under this MSA, so it doesn't need to be re-negotiated for each new SOW. Consulting IP is split into two categories, and getting this right protects both sides.
>
> **The consulting IP split:** **Consultant's pre-existing IP** — tools, frameworks, templates, methodologies, and know-how Consultant developed independently and brings to every client — stays Consultant's. **Work Product** — the specific deliverables created for Client under a particular SOW — becomes Client's once fully paid for.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** A single project-specific SOW quietly trying to claim ownership of Consultant's general methodology or reusable frameworks. If a Client's SOW template does this, it needs to be caught and negotiated — it directly conflicts with the IP split set here, which (per Section 2) should control unless a SOW clearly and specifically overrides it.

**5a. Consultant's Pre-existing IP.** Consultant retains all rights, title, and interest in its pre-existing tools, frameworks, templates, methodologies, processes, and proprietary know-how ("**Consultant IP**"), whether or not any Consultant IP is used or embedded in deliverables under any SOW. Nothing in this Agreement or any SOW transfers Consultant IP to Client unless a specific SOW explicitly says so.

**5b. Work Product Ownership.** All deliverables and work product created specifically for Client under a SOW ("**Work Product**") become Client's property upon full payment of all fees due for that SOW. Prior to full payment, Work Product remains Consultant's property.

**5c. Embedded Consultant IP — License.** To the extent Consultant IP is embedded in any Work Product, Consultant grants Client a non-exclusive, non-transferable license to use that embedded Consultant IP solely as part of the Work Product, for Client's internal business purposes.

## 8. Limitation of Liability

> **What this section does:** Caps how much either party can be forced to pay the other if something goes wrong, and excludes certain categories of damages (like lost profits) that are hard to predict and can be disproportionate to the size of the engagement. This is one of the most heavily negotiated clauses in any services contract — the cap protects a consulting business from being put out of business by a single lawsuit over a modest-fee engagement.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Signing a Client's standard vendor contract with no liability cap, or one with an "uncapped liability" clause. For a small consulting firm, uncapped liability on a $20,000 engagement can mean a single dispute wipes out the business. A cap tied to fees paid (commonly 12 months of fees, or the total contract value) is standard and reasonable for most engagements — carve-outs for things like confidentiality breaches or gross negligence are common and often expected by clients.
>
> *How to decide the cap: A common approach is capping each party's total liability at the fees paid (or payable) under the specific SOW giving rise to the claim over the preceding 12 months. Larger, higher-risk engagements sometimes negotiate a higher cap or specific carve-outs — that's a negotiation, not a fixed rule.*

Except for breaches of Section 5 (Intellectual Property) or Section 6 (Confidentiality), or claims arising from gross negligence or willful misconduct, neither party's total liability under this Agreement or any SOW will exceed {{liability_cap_description}}. Neither party will be liable for indirect, incidental, consequential, special, or punitive damages, including lost profits or lost data, even if advised of the possibility of such damages.

## 9. Indemnification

> **What this section does:** Sets out when one party has to cover the other's losses if a third party sues over something within that party's control — most commonly, a claim that Consultant's work product infringes someone else's IP, or a claim arising from either party's gross negligence.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Agreeing to an open-ended indemnification clause that covers claims outside your control. Indemnification should track responsibility — Consultant indemnifies for problems with its own work and IP; Client indemnifies for problems arising from Client's own materials, instructions, or misuse.

Consultant will indemnify and hold Client harmless from third-party claims that Consultant's Work Product, as delivered and used as intended, infringes a third party's intellectual property rights. Client will indemnify and hold Consultant harmless from third-party claims arising from Client's own materials, instructions, or Client's misuse of any deliverable. In either case, the indemnifying party's obligation is subject to the liability cap in Section 8, except for claims arising from gross negligence or willful misconduct.
