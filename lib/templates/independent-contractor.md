## 2. Timeline

> **What this section does:** Sets the time boundaries of this engagement — when it starts and when it ends (or how it ends if there's no fixed end date). This matters both for managing the relationship and for the worker classification analysis: a long-term, open-ended engagement looks more like employment than a defined project does.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Using the same contractor indefinitely on an open-ended agreement without ever revisiting the terms. Year after year of continuous, exclusive work — especially if the Company controls how and when the work is done — is one of the strongest indicators of an employment relationship, not a contractor relationship. If you're working with someone continuously for 12+ months, consult a lawyer about classification risk.
>
> *How to fill this in: Choose one: (a) fixed project — "Services will begin on [date] and conclude upon delivery and acceptance of [specific deliverable]"; (b) fixed term — "Services will begin on [start date] and continue through [end date]"; (c) ongoing — "Services will begin on [start date] and continue until terminated by either party as provided in Section 8."*

## 3. Payment

> **What this section does:** Defines the Contractor's compensation — the amount, timing, and invoicing process. A clear payment section prevents the two most common contractor disputes: "I didn't know when to invoice" and "I didn't know when I'd get paid."
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Not specifying a payment-due date. "Reasonable time" is not a payment term. Set a specific number of days (Net 15 or Net 30 are standard). Without a clear due date, the Contractor has no basis to claim a payment is late until a court decides what "reasonable" means.
>
> *How to decide hourly vs. fixed-price:*
> - *Hourly:* Fair when the scope is genuinely uncertain or likely to evolve. The Company pays for actual time; the Contractor gets paid for all work done. Requires the Contractor to track and submit hours.
> - *Fixed-price per deliverable:* Good when the scope is well-defined. The Company knows exactly what it will pay; the Contractor takes the risk if the work takes longer. Incentivizes efficiency but requires careful scope definition (see Section 1).
> - *Monthly retainer:* Good for ongoing part-time arrangements (e.g., 20 hours/month of design work). Simple to administer; works when you need reliable availability rather than specific deliverables.
>
> *Example: "Company will pay Contractor $85/hour for time logged on the Services. Contractor will submit invoices by the 1st of each month for hours worked in the prior month, and Company will pay each invoice within 15 days of receipt."*

## 4. Independent Contractor Status — Worker Classification

> **What this section does:** Establishes that the Contractor is not an employee — they are an independent business providing services. This has major legal consequences for taxes, benefits, and the Company's liability. But writing it in the contract is not enough on its own — the *actual working relationship* must also match an independent contractor arrangement. Courts and the IRS look at facts, not labels.
>
> **This is the single most important section in this agreement.** Misclassifying an employee as a contractor is one of the most expensive legal mistakes a startup can make. It can result in: back payroll taxes (the IRS will hold the Company liable for the employer's share), penalties and interest, state unemployment insurance and workers' comp liability, potential back pay for overtime and minimum wage violations, and class-action exposure if multiple workers were misclassified.
>
> **The three-factor test (IRS and most states):** Courts and agencies look at three categories of evidence:
>
> **(A) Behavioral control:** Does the Company control *how* the Contractor does the work — the methods, tools, process, schedule, and location? A contractor controls their own process. An employee follows the Company's instructions on how to work.
>
> **(B) Financial control:** Does the Contractor operate as an independent business? Signs of contractor status: works for multiple clients, can profit or lose money on a project, buys their own tools and equipment, is paid per project or invoice rather than hourly by the week. Signs of employee status: Company provides tools, worker has one exclusive "employer," is paid a regular salary regardless of output.
>
> **(C) Type of relationship:** Is the work central to the Company's business (not a good sign for contractor status), or a specialized skill the Company doesn't have in-house? Is there a written contract? Does the worker get benefits? Is the arrangement permanent or project-based?
>
> **The California AB5 warning:** California applies a much stricter test (the "ABC test") that presumes a worker is an employee unless the Company can prove three things. Many other states are moving toward similar tests. If the Contractor is based in California, or the work is performed in California, talk to a lawyer before signing this agreement — the standard contractor classification test may not apply.
>
> *Practical red flags: If you're telling the Contractor when to work, where to work, and exactly how to do the work — and they work exclusively for you — that's an employment relationship, regardless of what this contract says. The label doesn't control; the facts do.*

The Contractor is an **independent contractor**, not an employee, agent, or partner of the Company. The parties intend and agree that:

- The Contractor has the right to control *how* the Services are performed. The Company's interest is in the result, not the method.
- The Contractor uses their own tools, equipment, and work methods unless otherwise agreed.
- The Contractor is responsible for all federal, state, and local taxes on their compensation. The Company will not withhold income taxes, Social Security, Medicare, or unemployment taxes.
- The Contractor is not entitled to any employee benefits — including health insurance, retirement plans, paid leave, or stock options that require employee status.
- The Contractor may perform services for other clients during the term of this Agreement, provided this does not create a conflict of interest with the Company.
- Nothing in this Agreement shall be construed to create an employment, agency, joint venture, or partnership relationship.

## 5. Intellectual Property — Assignment and Pre-existing IP

> **What this section does:** Determines who owns the work the Contractor creates under this Agreement. This is the second most critical section. Without a clear IP assignment, the Contractor — not the Company — may own everything they built for you, even after you've paid them.
>
> **Why this is not automatic:** US copyright law's "work made for hire" doctrine automatically gives employers ownership of work created by employees within the scope of their employment. But it does *not* automatically apply to independent contractors. Under 17 U.S.C. § 101, contractor work only qualifies as "work made for hire" in nine specific, narrowly defined categories — most software code, marketing content, and design work does not fall into these categories. This means that without an explicit written assignment clause, the Contractor retains copyright ownership of everything they created — even if you paid them.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Removing or weakening the IP assignment clause to get a contractor to sign faster. This is a classic short-term/long-term tradeoff: the Contractor may push back on a broad IP assignment, but giving in can mean your product's core code, UI, or content is owned by someone who no longer works for you. Investors will find this in due diligence and it will cause serious problems. Keep the assignment clause — negotiate it if needed, but don't delete it.
>
> **The pre-existing IP carve-out (Section 5b) — important balance:** Some contractors bring their own pre-existing tools, libraries, frameworks, or code to the engagement. The assignment clause below covers *everything the Contractor creates under this Agreement* — including work that incorporates their pre-existing tools. To avoid inadvertently claiming ownership of a contractor's entire toolkit, Section 5b carves out their pre-existing IP and grants the Company a license to use it as embedded in the deliverable.

**5a. Assignment of New Work Product**

All work product, deliverables, inventions, code, designs, content, and materials created by the Contractor in connection with the Services under this Agreement ("Work Product") are deemed **work made for hire** to the fullest extent permitted by law. To the extent any Work Product does not qualify as work made for hire, the Contractor hereby **irrevocably assigns** to the Company all right, title, and interest in the Work Product, including all copyright, patent, trademark, trade secret, and other intellectual property rights worldwide.

The Contractor will execute any additional documents the Company reasonably requests to evidence, perfect, or record this assignment — at no additional cost.

*Why "to the extent permitted by law" matters: Not all types of work can be work-made-for-hire for a contractor (only those 9 categories). The dual approach above — work-for-hire first, assignment as backup — is the belt-and-suspenders standard language. Keep both.*

**5b. Contractor's Pre-existing IP**

The Contractor retains ownership of any tools, frameworks, libraries, code, processes, or materials created independently before this Agreement and brought to the engagement ("Pre-existing IP"). If Contractor incorporates Pre-existing IP into the Work Product, the Contractor grants the Company a **non-exclusive, royalty-free, perpetual license** to use that Pre-existing IP solely as embedded in the Work Product — the Company may not use the Pre-existing IP independently of the Work Product without the Contractor's written consent.

*How to fill this in: If the Contractor has specific pre-existing tools or libraries that will be used in the project, list them here — for example, "Contractor's custom React component library developed prior to [date]." This prevents disputes about what is and isn't covered by the assignment.*

## 7. Non-Solicitation (Optional)

> **What this section does:** Prevents the Contractor from poaching the Company's employees or clients for a period after the engagement ends. This section is *optional* — include it if the Contractor will have access to the Company's key employees or client relationships.
>
> **Note on non-compete clauses:** Many states limit or prohibit non-compete agreements. California bans them almost entirely for employees — and the rules for contractors are similarly strict in several states. Non-*solicitation* clauses (which only prevent poaching, not competing) are enforced more broadly, but still vary by state. Do not add a non-compete clause to this agreement without consulting a lawyer — it may be unenforceable or, in some states, create liability for the Company.
>
> *How to fill this in: 12–18 months is a typical non-solicitation period. Include this section only if it's relevant to your situation — there's no need to add provisions for scenarios that won't arise.*

## 8. Termination

> **What this section does:** Defines how either side can end this Agreement and what happens when they do — specifically, who owes what for work already done. A clean termination clause prevents the most common end-of-engagement dispute: the Contractor saying they're owed for work-in-progress that the Company says it doesn't want.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Not specifying that the Company will pay for work properly completed before termination. "Termination with notice" means the Company must pay for Services actually performed and properly invoiced through the notice period — not a refund of anything already paid.
>
> *How to set the notice period: 14–30 days is standard for most contractor engagements. Use a shorter period (7–10 days) for short-term projects. Use a longer period (30 days) if the Contractor needs significant time to transition work or hand off to someone new.*
