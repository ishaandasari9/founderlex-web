## ARTICLE II — BOARD OF DIRECTORS

> **What this article does:** Defines how the Organization is governed — who the directors are, how they are chosen, how long they serve, how they can be removed, and what voting power they hold. The Board of Directors is the legal governing body of the Organization; it is ultimately responsible for everything the Organization does.

**Section 2.1 — Role and authority.** The Board of Directors (the "Board") is the supreme governing body of the Organization. The Board is responsible for: setting strategic direction; approving the annual budget; hiring and evaluating the Executive Director (if any); ensuring compliance with legal requirements; and acting as fiduciaries on behalf of the Organization's charitable mission.

> *Fiduciary duties in plain English: Every board member owes the Organization three duties — (1) **Duty of Care:** be an engaged, informed decision-maker; (2) **Duty of Loyalty:** put the Organization's interests before your own; (3) **Duty of Obedience:** act consistent with the Organization's stated mission and Bylaws. Breaching these duties can expose a director to personal liability.*

**Section 2.2 — Number of directors.** The Board shall consist of not fewer than **{{board_min_directors}}** nor more than **{{board_max_directors}}** directors. The exact number within this range shall be set by Board resolution. A majority of the directors must be independent — meaning they have no material financial relationship with the Organization other than as donors.

> *Why independence matters: The IRS scrutinizes board independence on Form 1023. A board dominated by founders, family members, or paid staff raises concerns about private inurement. Structure the board so a majority of seats are held by people with no financial relationship to the Organization or to one another.*

**Section 2.3 — Terms.** Each director shall serve a term of **{{director_term_years}} year(s)**, beginning at the meeting at which they are elected. Directors may serve a maximum of **{{director_max_consecutive_terms}}** consecutive terms. After reaching the maximum, a director may be re-elected after sitting out at least one full term.

> *How to set terms: 2-year staggered terms are the standard for most nonprofits — they ensure continuity (not everyone cycles off at once) while preventing any one group from entrenching permanently. A term limit of 2–3 consecutive terms is best practice.*

**Section 2.4 — Election.** Directors shall be elected by a majority vote of the then-seated Board at any duly noticed Board meeting. Initial directors are identified in the Articles of Incorporation.

**Section 2.5 — Vacancies.** Vacancies on the Board (due to resignation, removal, death, or any other reason) shall be filled by a majority vote of the remaining directors, even if less than a quorum. A director elected to fill a vacancy shall serve for the remainder of the unexpired term.

**Section 2.6 — Resignation.** Any director may resign by delivering written notice to the Chair or Secretary. Resignation is effective upon receipt unless a later effective date is specified.

**Section 2.7 — Removal.** A director may be removed, with or without cause, by a **two-thirds (2/3) vote** of all directors then serving, at any duly noticed meeting at which a quorum is present, provided that the notice of the meeting states the removal as a purpose.

> *Why a supermajority for removal: Requiring two-thirds prevents any faction of the board from easily removing a director they simply disagree with. It raises the bar to serious cause or sustained concern.*

**Section 2.8 — Compensation.** Directors shall not receive compensation for their service as directors. The Board may, however, approve reimbursement of reasonable, documented expenses incurred in connection with Board service.

## ARTICLE III — MEETINGS OF THE BOARD

> **What this article does:** Establishes how and when the Board meets, how meetings are noticed, what constitutes a quorum, and how business is conducted. A nonprofit that doesn't keep consistent, documented meeting records risks losing legitimacy with the IRS, grantors, and auditors.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Not keeping written minutes. Meeting minutes are a legal record. The IRS, state regulators, and major grantors can ask for them. Keep minutes of every Board meeting from the very first one, and store them permanently.

**Section 3.1 — Regular meetings.** The Board shall hold at least **{{regular_meeting_frequency}}** regular meetings per year. The dates, times, and locations shall be set by the Board at the beginning of each calendar year.

**Section 3.2 — Special meetings.** Special meetings of the Board may be called by the Chair, the Executive Director (if any), or any **{{special_meeting_director_count}}** directors upon at least **{{special_meeting_notice_days}} days'** written notice to all directors.

**Section 3.3 — Notice.** Notice of all Board meetings shall be given to each director at least **{{regular_notice_days}} days** in advance. Notice may be given in writing (including email). Notice may be waived in writing by any director before or after the meeting.

**Section 3.4 — Quorum.** A quorum for the transaction of business shall be a **majority** of the directors then serving. No business may be conducted at any meeting at which a quorum is not present, except the adjournment of the meeting.

> *Why quorum matters: Without quorum rules, a small subset of directors could make decisions that bind the entire Organization. Requiring a majority ensures decisions reflect the Board as a whole.*
>
> *Example: If the Board has 7 directors, a quorum is 4. If 4 directors are present and a vote passes 3-1, it is a valid Board action.*

**Section 3.5 — Voting.** Each director has one vote. Decisions shall be made by a majority vote of directors present at a meeting at which a quorum exists, unless a higher threshold is specified elsewhere in these Bylaws.

**Section 3.6 — Remote meetings.** Directors may participate in any Board meeting by telephone, video conference, or other electronic means that allows all participants to hear one another simultaneously. Participation in this manner constitutes presence at the meeting.

**Section 3.7 — Action without a meeting.** Any action required or permitted to be taken at a Board meeting may be taken without a meeting if all directors sign a written consent authorizing the action. Written consent may be transmitted by email. Such consent shall be filed with the minutes of the Board.

> *When to use: Written consent (sometimes called "unanimous written consent" or "UWC") is useful for routine approvals that don't require full discussion — signing a contract, approving minutes, adding a bank signatory. Reserve formal meetings for substantive decisions.*

**Section 3.8 — Minutes.** The Secretary shall keep written minutes of all Board meetings. Minutes shall record: date, time, and location; names of directors present and absent; a summary of matters discussed; all motions made and whether passed or failed; and any conflict of interest disclosures. Minutes shall be approved at the next Board meeting and kept permanently in the Organization's records.

## ARTICLE IV — OFFICERS

> **What this article does:** Creates the officer roles — the individuals with day-to-day authority to sign documents, manage finances, and run meetings. Officers are typically elected by the Board, not the public.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Having the founder serve simultaneously as Board Chair and Executive Director. These roles have a supervisory relationship — the Board Chair oversees the Executive Director. When one person holds both roles, there is no meaningful oversight. For a new organization without a paid executive director yet, the founder can serve as Chair (a volunteer board leadership role) and also manage operations — but separate the roles formally as soon as the organization grows.

**Section 4.1 — Officers.** The officers of the Organization shall be: a **Chair** (or President), a **Vice Chair** (or Vice President), a **Secretary**, and a **Treasurer**. The Board may create additional officer positions by resolution.

**Section 4.2 — Election and terms.** Officers shall be elected by the Board at the annual meeting of the Board (or at a special meeting called for that purpose). Each officer shall serve a term of **{{officer_term_years}} year(s)** and may be re-elected.

**Section 4.3 — Removal.** Any officer may be removed by a majority vote of the Board at any meeting at which a quorum is present.

**Section 4.4 — Chair.** The Chair presides at all meetings of the Board. The Chair is the primary liaison between the Board and the Executive Director (if any). The Chair signs official documents on behalf of the Organization when authorized by the Board.

**Section 4.5 — Vice Chair.** The Vice Chair performs the duties of the Chair when the Chair is absent or unable to act, and such other duties as assigned by the Chair or Board.

**Section 4.6 — Secretary.** The Secretary keeps the minutes of all Board meetings; maintains official records and files; sends required notices of meetings; and maintains custody of the corporate seal (if any). The Secretary ensures that all required state filings (annual reports, registered agent updates) are made on time.

> *Why the Secretary role matters: The Secretary is the Organization's official record-keeper. In the event of an IRS inquiry, a grant audit, or a legal dispute, accurate minutes and records maintained by the Secretary are the primary evidence that the Organization was run properly.*

**Section 4.7 — Treasurer.** The Treasurer oversees the financial affairs of the Organization; ensures accurate financial records are maintained; presents a financial report to the Board at each regular meeting; ensures Form 990 and other tax filings are completed on time; and works with any outside accountant or auditor. The Treasurer co-signs (with the Chair) any check, wire, or financial transaction above ${{treasurer_cosign_threshold}}.

> *Setting the co-signature threshold: For a new nonprofit, requiring two signatures (Chair + Treasurer) on transactions above $500–$1,000 is standard internal financial control. It prevents any one person from misappropriating funds and demonstrates financial controls to grantors and the IRS.*

## ARTICLE V — COMMITTEES

> **What this article does:** Allows the Board to create standing or ad hoc committees to handle specific areas of work (fundraising, programs, finance, audit). Committees can do research and make recommendations but generally cannot make final decisions on behalf of the full Board without Board authorization.

**Section 5.1 — Standing committees.** The Board may establish standing committees by resolution. Each standing committee shall have at least two Board members; committees may also include non-Board members. Committees shall report their activities to the Board at each regular meeting.

**Section 5.2 — Executive Committee.** The Board may establish an Executive Committee consisting of the Chair, Vice Chair, Secretary, and Treasurer. Between Board meetings, the Executive Committee may act on routine matters that cannot wait for the next Board meeting, subject to ratification by the full Board at its next meeting. The Executive Committee may not take any action that: amends the Bylaws; removes a director; approves a budget; or is otherwise reserved for the full Board.

**Section 5.3 — Advisory boards.** The Board may establish one or more advisory boards of non-directors to provide expertise, connections, and support. Advisory board members have no voting authority and no fiduciary duties.

## ARTICLE VI — CONFLICT OF INTEREST

> **What this article does:** Creates the basic Bylaw-level commitment to managing conflicts of interest. This section works in tandem with the Organization's standalone Conflict of Interest Policy (a separate document the IRS requires). This Bylaw section gives the Conflict of Interest Policy its formal authority.

**Section 6.1 — Policy required.** The Organization shall maintain and adhere to a written Conflict of Interest Policy, which the Board shall review and reaffirm annually. The Conflict of Interest Policy is incorporated into these Bylaws by reference.

**Section 6.2 — Annual disclosure.** Each director and officer shall complete a written annual disclosure questionnaire identifying any potential conflicts of interest. Completed questionnaires shall be filed with the Secretary and kept with the Board minutes.

**Section 6.3 — Recusal.** Any director or officer with a conflict of interest (as defined in the Conflict of Interest Policy) in a matter before the Board must: disclose the conflict before discussion begins; leave the room during Board deliberation on the matter; and refrain from voting on the matter. The conflict and recusal shall be recorded in the meeting minutes.

## ARTICLE VII — FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

> **What this article does:** Establishes basic financial controls — the fiscal year, budget approval, banking authority, and audit requirements. These provisions demonstrate to grantors and the IRS that the Organization manages money responsibly.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Allowing a single person to have sole control over the Organization's bank accounts with no Board oversight. This is the most common route to financial mismanagement in small nonprofits. The co-signature requirement for large transactions and the Treasurer's reporting obligation to the Board are the basic safeguards.

**Section 7.1 — Fiscal year.** The fiscal year of the Organization shall begin on **{{fiscal_year_start}}** and end on **{{fiscal_year_end}}** of each year.

**Section 7.2 — Budget.** The Board shall adopt an annual operating budget before or at the start of each fiscal year. Material deviations from the approved budget require Board approval.

**Section 7.3 — Banking.** The Organization shall maintain one or more bank accounts in its legal name. The Board shall authorize specific individuals (typically the Chair and Treasurer) to open accounts and sign checks. Any transaction above ${{large_transaction_threshold}} shall require the signature or approval of two authorized signatories.

**Section 7.4 — Financial review and audit.** The Board shall ensure that accurate financial records are maintained. At the close of each fiscal year, the Treasurer shall prepare or oversee preparation of financial statements. Organizations with gross receipts above $[state-specific threshold — confirm with attorney] should engage an independent accountant for a review or audit. Major grantors often require audited financial statements above certain funding levels.

**Section 7.5 — Form 990.** The Treasurer (or designated accountant) shall ensure the Organization files the appropriate Form 990 (990-N, 990-EZ, or 990) with the IRS by the deadline — the 15th day of the 5th month after the close of the fiscal year. **Missing three consecutive 990 filings results in automatic revocation of 501(c)(3) status.** Put this deadline on a permanent recurring calendar reminder.

## ARTICLE VIII — INDEMNIFICATION

> **What this article does:** Protects directors, officers, and employees from personal financial liability when they act in good faith on behalf of the Organization. Without an indemnification provision, qualified people may be unwilling to serve on the Board — the personal liability exposure is too high. This provision tells directors: if you act in good faith and something goes wrong, the Organization will defend you and cover your costs.
>
> *Limits: Indemnification typically does not cover intentional misconduct, actions taken for personal benefit, or criminal acts. It covers honest mistakes and reasonable business judgments that turn out to be wrong.*

The Organization shall indemnify and hold harmless its directors, officers, employees, and agents (each an "Indemnified Person") against all claims, liabilities, losses, damages, and expenses (including reasonable attorneys' fees) incurred in connection with any proceeding arising out of the Indemnified Person's service to the Organization, to the fullest extent permitted by the laws of the State of {{state_of_incorporation}} — provided that the Indemnified Person: (a) acted in good faith; (b) acted in a manner reasonably believed to be in the best interests of the Organization; and (c) had no reasonable cause to believe their conduct was unlawful.

The Organization may purchase and maintain directors' and officers' (D&O) liability insurance to support this indemnification obligation.

## ARTICLE IX — AMENDMENTS

> **What this section does:** Establishes how these Bylaws can be changed. A supermajority requirement (two-thirds) for Bylaw amendments prevents any small faction of the Board from unilaterally rewriting the rules that govern everyone. Advance written notice ensures all directors have time to review proposed changes before voting.
>
> *Common mistake to avoid: Making Bylaws unamendable without unanimous consent. A 100% requirement to amend means any single director can block changes the rest of the board agrees are needed — this creates dysfunction as the organization evolves. Two-thirds is the right balance.*

These Bylaws may be amended by an affirmative vote of **two-thirds (2/3)** of all directors then serving, provided that:
- Written notice of the proposed amendment is distributed to all directors at least **{{amendment_notice_days}} days** before the meeting at which the vote will occur.
- The notice includes the proposed amendment text in full.

Bylaw amendments take effect immediately upon adoption unless the amendment itself specifies a later effective date.

## ARTICLE X — DISSOLUTION

> **What this section does:** Establishes the procedure for winding down the Organization and distributing its assets if it ever dissolves. This section must be consistent with the dissolution clause in the Articles of Incorporation — assets must go to another 501(c)(3) or a government entity, never to the founders or board members.

Dissolution of the Organization requires approval by a **two-thirds (2/3)** vote of all directors then serving, plus compliance with all applicable state nonprofit dissolution procedures (typically: filing Articles of Dissolution with the state Secretary of State; notifying creditors; settling outstanding obligations).

Upon dissolution, after paying all debts and obligations, all remaining assets shall be distributed in accordance with the dissolution clause in the Organization's Articles of Incorporation — exclusively to organizations qualifying under Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, or to the federal, state, or local government for a public purpose.
