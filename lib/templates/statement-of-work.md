## 6. Payment Terms

> **What this section does:** Defines exactly how and when the Consultant gets paid — billing method, schedule, invoice process, and consequences for late payment. Clear payment terms prevent the single most common consulting business problem: late or disputed invoices.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Net 30 with no late-payment consequence. Without a late-fee clause, a Client has no financial incentive to pay on time. Include a monthly interest charge and — critically — the right to pause work if an invoice goes overdue. Pausing work is your most practical leverage.
>
> *How to decide your billing method:*
> - *Hourly:* Simple. You bill for actual time. Good when scope is uncertain.
> - *Fixed-price:* You quote a total number and stick to it. You take the risk if the work takes longer; the Client has price certainty.
> - *Milestone-based:* A variation of fixed-price where payments are tied to deliverables (e.g., 30% on signing, 40% at mid-project, 30% at final delivery). Good for cash flow on both sides.
> - *Retainer:* A fixed monthly fee for ongoing advisory. Good for long-term relationships.
>

## 7. Change Orders

> **What this section does:** Establishes the process for handling work outside the original scope of this SOW. This is the single most important operational clause in any consulting contract — it is what prevents the slow, silent expansion of scope that erodes margins without anyone explicitly agreeing to more work.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Doing the extra work first, then trying to bill for it. Once you have delivered the work, you have lost most of your leverage. The discipline is: when a Client asks for something that is outside scope, stop and respond in writing *before* doing anything. A simple reply — "Happy to help with that — it's outside our current SOW, so I'll send a short Change Order before we start" — sets the right expectation professionally and without conflict.
>
> *How to set the change-order rate: It can match the rates in Section 5, or be higher — some consultants charge a premium for scope changes because they disrupt the engagement rhythm and require re-planning.*

## 8. Intellectual Property

> **What this section does:** Defines who owns what IP after the engagement ends. This is the most legally significant section in the SOW. The rules are different from a standard contractor or employment relationship — and getting it wrong can mean inadvertently giving away the Consultant's competitive methodology, or leaving the Client unsure whether they actually own what they paid for.
>
> **The consulting IP split — understand this before signing anything:**
> Two categories of IP exist in every consulting engagement, and they are treated differently:
>
> **Category 1 — Consultant's pre-existing IP (Consultant keeps this):** Tools, frameworks, templates, models, methodologies, and know-how the Consultant developed independently and brings to every client engagement. This is the Consultant's competitive advantage — the thing that makes them good at what they do. It cannot be transferred to a single client without destroying the Consultant's ability to serve other clients with the same expertise.
>
> **Category 2 — Work product (Client owns this):** The specific deliverables created for this Client, for this engagement, paid for under this SOW. The analysis, the strategy deck, the financial model — all of it belongs to the Client once they have paid for it.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Signing a Client's standard vendor contract without reading the IP clause. Large company procurement contracts often contain a blanket IP assignment that transfers *everything* to the Client — including your pre-existing methodology. Always read and amend any IP clause before signing. Add "Notwithstanding the foregoing, Consultant retains all rights to its pre-existing tools, frameworks, and methodologies" if the Client's contract doesn't already say this.
>
> *Note on "upon full payment": The Work Product transfers to the Client only after all invoices are paid in full. Until then, the Consultant retains ownership. This protects the Consultant if payment is disputed.*

## 10. Termination

> **What this section does:** Defines how either party can end the engagement early and what happens to money and deliverables when they do. Without this clause, either party could argue they are locked in forever — or that they can walk away with no notice and no obligation to pay for work already done.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Not specifying that the Client owes for work already completed. On early termination, the Client owes for all work performed and expenses incurred up to the termination date. Make this explicit — "work in progress" payment is a common dispute point, especially if the Client terminates because they didn't like where the project was heading.
