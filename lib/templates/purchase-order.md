## 1. What Is a Purchase Order — and When to Use One

> **What this section does:** This is an educational note, not legal language — it stays in the document so you understand what you're looking at.
>
> **A Purchase Order is a formal written offer from a Buyer to a Vendor** to purchase specific goods or services at a stated price. When the Vendor accepts the PO (by signing it, sending a confirmation, or beginning work), it becomes a binding contract.
>
> **Use a PO when:**
> - You are buying goods or services from a vendor and want a clear, documented record of exactly what was ordered and at what price
> - Your internal financial controls or accounting system require PO numbers before you can pay an invoice
> - You want to formally authorize a transaction that was previously discussed verbally or by email
> - You're a larger company and a corporate Client is sending you a PO as part of their procurement process
>
> **How a PO relates to a Statement of Work (SOW):**
> A PO and an SOW are complementary, not interchangeable. An SOW governs the *terms* of a service engagement — scope, IP, confidentiality, termination, governing law. A PO authorizes a *specific transaction* within that relationship — it's a financial instrument, not a service agreement. For anything beyond a simple goods purchase, you want both: an SOW for the terms and a PO for the financial authorization.
>
> **Critical watch-out:** If a corporate Client sends you their own PO with their standard vendor terms on it, read it carefully before signing. Corporate POs often include terms that could conflict with your SOW — including IP clauses, payment terms, and liability limits. The rule is: your SOW governs; the PO is for the financial authorization only. If the Client's PO says otherwise, address it in writing before accepting.

## 4. Payment Terms

> **What this section does:** Specifies when and how the Buyer will pay the Vendor after the goods or services are received. This should match the payment terms in any underlying SOW or contract. If it doesn't match, you need to resolve the conflict in writing before this PO is accepted.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Setting different payment terms in the PO vs. the SOW. If the SOW says Net 15 and the PO says Net 30, you have a conflict — the Vendor doesn't know when to expect payment, and you don't know which terms control. Keep them consistent or specify clearly which document governs payment timing.
>
> *Standard payment terms: "Net 15" means payment is due 15 days after invoice date. "Net 30" is 30 days. "Due on receipt" means immediately. For a small vendor or contractor you want to pay quickly, use Net 15 — it builds goodwill and the Vendor will deprioritize slower-paying clients.*

## 5. Relationship to Underlying Agreement

> **What this section does:** Establishes that this PO is a financial authorization only — it doesn't replace or modify the terms of any underlying SOW, contract, or Master Services Agreement. This is the most important protective clause in the PO if you're using it alongside an SOW.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Accepting a corporate Client's PO that says "this PO supersedes all prior agreements." That language would override your SOW — including your IP clause, your rate schedule, your change-order process, and your governing law. Never accept a PO with "supersedes" language without amending it. The correct relationship is: the SOW governs terms; the PO authorizes a specific financial transaction.
>
> *If the Vendor (that's you) receives a Client PO: check for the following red flags before accepting: (1) IP clause that transfers all rights to Client without your carve-outs; (2) payment terms that differ from your SOW; (3) "supersedes all prior agreements" language; (4) governing law that differs from your SOW. Flag any conflict to the Client in writing before beginning work.*

This Purchase Order is issued pursuant to and subject to the terms of **{{reference_document}}** (the "Underlying Agreement") between the parties. In the event of any conflict between the terms of this PO and the Underlying Agreement:

*Choose the option that fits your situation and delete the other. Option A protects the Vendor who already has a favorable SOW. Option B is used when the Buyer wants the PO's specific commercial terms to take precedence for this transaction.*

## 6. Acceptance

>
> *Why this matters: A PO is an offer. The Vendor's acceptance creates the contract. If the Vendor begins work without any acknowledgment, acceptance may be inferred from conduct — but having a written confirmation is cleaner and avoids ambiguity.*
