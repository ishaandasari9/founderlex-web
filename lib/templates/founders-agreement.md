## 3. Equity Ownership

> **What this section does:** Locks in who owns what percentage of the company, in writing, before any money changes hands. Equity is ownership — if the company is ever sold or raises investment, these percentages determine what each founder receives. A verbal equity agreement is nearly impossible to enforce.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Splitting 100% between the founders with nothing left over. Most companies eventually want to give equity to key early employees or set aside shares for future investors. Consider reserving 10–20% as an "option pool." If you don't plan for it now, that pool will come out of your existing shares later — at a moment when you have less bargaining power.
>
> *How to decide the split: Consider each founder's contribution of the original idea, technical work, and capital; their time commitment (full-time vs. part-time); and who is taking on the CEO role and its extra accountability. A 60/40 split is common when one person is clearly the lead; 50/50 when both founders are genuinely equal — but add a tiebreaker for deadlocks (see Section 4).*
>
> *Example: Founder 1 (technical lead, full-time) — 55%. Founder 2 (business lead, full-time) — 35%. Reserved option pool for future hires — 10%. Total: 100%.*

## 5. Vesting

> **What this section does:** Vesting means founders *earn* their equity over time, rather than owning it all on day one. If a co-founder leaves early, they only keep the shares they've already vested — the rest returns to the company.
>
> **Why this matters:** Without vesting, a co-founder who quits after two months can walk away with 40% or 50% of the company, leaving the remaining founders to do all the work while that person holds a large chunk of the equity and its future value. Vesting protects everyone — including you, if your own life circumstances change.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid:** Setting the vesting start date to the date you're signing this agreement, even though you've been working together for months already. You can set a vesting *commencement date* that's earlier than the signing date to reflect the time already invested. Just agree on it explicitly and write it here.
>
> *How to decide: A 4-year schedule with a 1-year cliff is the most common setup in early-stage startups. The cliff means: if someone leaves before the cliff date, they get nothing. After the cliff, equity vests in equal monthly installments for the remaining years.*
>
> *Example timeline: Vesting starts October 1, 2024 (backdated). Cliff: October 1, 2025 — no equity before this date. After the cliff, 1/48th of each founder's equity vests every month. Fully vested: October 1, 2028.*
>
> **The 83(b) election — do not miss this deadline:** When you receive equity subject to vesting, the IRS can tax each vesting event as income at the shares' value at that time. If the company grows, that means a large, unexpected tax bill every month. Filing an **83(b) election** within **30 days of signing** tells the IRS you want to be taxed on the shares' value *today* (usually near zero), not later when they vest. **Missing this 30-day window cannot be undone.** Ask a lawyer or accountant about this before you sign.

- **Vesting schedule:** {{vesting_years}} years total
- **Cliff:** {{cliff_months}} months — no equity vests before this date
- **Vesting commencement date:** [TO BE COMPLETED: the date vesting begins — can be backdated to when founders started working together]
- Unvested equity is forfeited and returns to the Company if a founder departs before it vests.

## 6. Intellectual Property Assignment

> **What this section does:** Transfers ownership of everything each founder creates for the company — code, designs, content, inventions, the company name, everything — to the *company itself*, not to any individual founder. This is what ensures the company, not a person, owns what was built.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid — forgetting pre-agreement work:** Founders often build the core product before they sign any agreement. Without explicitly covering "work created before this Agreement was signed," the founder who built the prototype may technically own it in their personal name. This clause covers past and future work. After signing, also move company assets (code repositories, domain names, cloud accounts) into company-owned accounts — not personal ones.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid — assuming this is optional:** Investors specifically check for IP assignment when doing due diligence before writing a check. If a co-founder's IP was never formally assigned to the company, the deal can fall apart or be delayed by months while the issue gets fixed. Do not remove or water down this clause without explicit legal advice.

**Each founder assigns to the Company all intellectual property** they create related to the Company's business — including code, designs, inventions, content, trademarks, trade secrets, and other work — whether created before or after the date of this Agreement. This ensures the Company, not any individual, owns what is built.

Each founder will sign any additional documents the Company reasonably requests to confirm or record this assignment.

## 9. What Happens If a Founder Leaves

> **What this section does:** Describes the consequences when a founder exits — whether voluntarily (they quit) or involuntarily (the other founders vote them out). This is the hardest section to write because it feels pessimistic, but it is the most important section to have if things go wrong.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid — not specifying Day 1 access:** On the day a founder departs, what happens to their access to the codebase, the company email, the bank account, the social media logins? Without a clear answer in writing, this becomes a tense, chaotic negotiation at the worst possible time. Add an "immediate actions on departure" clause that specifies what gets revoked, when, and who handles it.
>
> **Common mistake to avoid — ignoring "good leaver vs. bad leaver":** The consequences of departure can reasonably differ depending on *why* someone left. Being fired without cause is different from quitting, which is different from being fired for stealing from the company. A lawyer can help you define these categories and their different consequences — this is one section where legal help is especially worth the cost.
>

## 10. Dispute Resolution

> **What this section does:** Decides in advance how you'll handle disagreements — before you're in the middle of one. It's much easier to agree on a fair process when everyone is getting along.
>
> *How to fill this in — your options from informal to formal:*
> - *Good-faith discussion (free):* Try to resolve it yourselves first. Set a time limit (e.g., 30 days) so it doesn't drag on.
> - *Mediation ($500–$2,000):* A neutral third party helps you reach an agreement. Not binding — either side can walk away.
> - *Binding arbitration ($3,000–$10,000):* A private judge makes a final, enforceable decision. Much faster and cheaper than court.
> - *Litigation (most expensive, slowest):* Courts. Usually the last resort.
>
> *Example: "Disputes will first be addressed through good-faith discussion for up to 30 days. If unresolved, the parties agree to non-binding mediation. If mediation fails, disputes will be resolved by binding arbitration under JAMS rules in the State of [Delaware/your state]."*

Disputes between founders will first be addressed through good-faith discussion for up to [TO BE COMPLETED: e.g., 30] days.
